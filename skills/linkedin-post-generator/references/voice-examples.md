## 1. Target-lane posts (match these — topic AND voice)

These are user-edited. They define the lane: contrarian, data-led, ecommerce analytics, built from a finding. Note the lowercase, the plain language, the real number mechanics, the breakdown blocks, and that closes vary between takeaway and question.

### Target post A — [hook: blunt opinion | body: one argument built out | close: takeaway]

most ecommerce brands don't have a marketing problem. they have a retention problem they keep mislabeling.

i've looked at a lot of businesses where the owner is convinced they need more traffic, sales channels, and ad spend. then you pull the numbers and first-time buyers are converting fine, but they never come back.

when the majority of your revenue comes from new customers, you're forced to repeatedly pay the full customer acquisition cost.

research consistently shows that acquiring a new customer is approximately 5–7x more expensive than retaining an existing one, and in some industries the gap can exceed 20–25x.

as your business shifts toward repeat customers, your blended marketing cost per sale falls because a much larger share of revenue is generated without paying to acquire a brand-new customer each time.

for example, if it costs $50 in advertising to acquire a new customer, that $50 must be absorbed by the first purchase. if the average customer makes five purchases over their lifetime, that same $50 is spread across multiple sales, reducing the acquisition cost per sale to around $10.

1 purchase > $50 acquisition cost per sale
5 purchases > $10 acquisition cost per sale
10 purchases > $5 acquisition cost per sale

stop buying new customers when you can tap into your existing ones.

### Target post B — [hook: surprising stat | body: what most do vs what works | close: question]

a company i worked with was spending thousands of dollars per month on ads and couldn't tell me which products actually made them money.

this is more normal than you'd think.

most teams track revenue by channel, celebrate when the top-line number goes up, and assume the product mix is fine because sales are growing.

but you need track contribution margin by product, not revenue. revenue tells you what sells. contribution margin tells you what makes money.

once you factor in shipping, returns, fulfillment, and other variable costs, the highest-selling product isn't always the most profitable, and a lower-volume product can contribute far more to overall profit.

don't scale ads on the wrong product for months.

do you have contribution margin by product in your shopify dashboard?

### Target post C — [hook: common belief, then knock it down | body: short example | close: takeaway]

"we're data-driven" is one of the most overused phrases in ecommerce.

most brands that say this just have dashboards. they log in a few times a week, look at the charts, and move on.

having data and using data are two different things.

don't ask what happened this week, ask what decision should i make based on this data.

should we increase ad spend, pause a campaign, raise prices, reorder inventory, or launch a new product?

if your dashboard doesn't help you answer questions like these, it's just reporting.

analytics should reduce uncertainty and every dashboard should end with a decision.

## 2. Voice anchors (borrow the cadence, NOT the topic)

These older posts are about data careers — a topic the skill does not write about. They're here only because they show the user's natural first-person voice at its strongest: blunt, casual, matter-of-fact, unbothered.

Notice the cadence in lines like these (paraphrased markers to study, not to reuse):
- treating a pushback as obvious and dismissing it plainly ("ok so what? this is a business and that is a business expense.")
- walking through real personal numbers without dressing them up (proposal counts, rates dropped from $15/hr down and down, connects costing ~$3 each)
- short declarative momentum, no hype, occasional dry confidence

The user's two best-reaching real posts were clean "here's the breakdown" reference posts with numbered roles. Two takeaways:
- **breakdown/numbered structure drives reach** — reach for it.
- but those breakdown posts were written in a flatter, more generic voice (see anti-anchor). The win is to keep the **breakdown structure** while writing in the **blunt first-person voice** of the personal posts.

## 3. Anti-anchor (do NOT write like this)

The user has explicitly moved away from this tone. It's a generic, slightly corporate explainer voice — capitalized role names, neutral descriptions, no opinion, no edge. Avoid all of it.

Markers of the BAD tone to avoid:
- Capitalized Title Case Role Names and headers
- neutral, encyclopedic descriptions with no point of view ("designs, builds, and manages data pipelines...")
- filler intros like "the cloud has revolutionized how organizations manage, process, and analyze data"
- listicles that inform but take no stance and pick no fight
- generic engagement-bait closes like "which role excites you most? let's discuss in the comments" with nothing contrarian behind them

If a draft reads like a neutral explainer that could've come from any content agency, it's wrong. Add a stance, cut the filler, lowercase it, and make it argue something.
